# Knowledge base — the digital home framework

Distilled from the Brave Brand video **"Websites Are Dead. Build THIS Instead"**
(Luke, Brave Brand). This file is the skill's single source of video knowledge
for audits, recommendations, and Q&A.

**Provenance rule:** every figure below is *as cited by the presenter* — the
video quotes studies and sources but the skill does not independently verify
them. Always present statistics with that label ("as cited in the video").
When answering beyond this file, say so explicitly and label it "general
knowledge" or "not covered by the video".

## 1. Core thesis and definitions

**Digital brochure** — a website that only describes the business: homepage,
about, services, contact. "A sign in the window with nothing to buy." It tells
people the business exists but gives them no way to spend money and no reason to
stay in touch.

**Digital home** — one owned, central place (the website) with many doors
leading into it: every collection point lives under the home, each page has one
job, and all data flows back to the owner.

**Digital homelessness** — living on rented platforms (social feeds, marketplaces):
having an audience but no owned relationship. The platform owns the followers,
the data, and the relationship; the landlord sets the rules and can lock the
account out overnight (this happened to one of the presenter's clients).

**Roads vs home** — platforms (YouTube, Instagram, X, Google, AI answers) are
*roads* that lead people past the door. Rent the roads for traffic all day long
— but don't live on the road in a tent. Renting the store: Amazon sellers paid
~$172B in marketplace fees + ~$68B in ads (~$200B/yr) for their spot.

**Why most websites make no money** — not because websites are dead, but
because most were *never built to collect anything*. The building is easy;
turning a profit is the hard part.

**The landscape (as cited in the video):** ~1.5 billion websites exist; ~85%
are inactive (parked/abandoned); of the active ones, roughly 1 in 100 makes
real money. ~118 million websites were added January–July of the video's year
(~650,000/day). Picture a high street of 100 shops: 85 boarded up, 14 window
displays with nothing inside, 1 actually open for business.

## 2. Collection points

**Definition** (via the presenter's mentor Paul Counsel): people will spend
their money somewhere regardless; a *collection point* is simply a way for
someone to spend money with you. A flat white and a latte are the same
collection point; food is a different one; merch is another; the sauna is
another.

**The cafe test:** empty cafes have 4–5 collection points; the packed cafe
(Open House, Bali) has 30+ per location — so people come in the morning, at
lunch, and again for dinner. More ways to spend money = more reasons to return
= longevity.

**Online forms of collection points:** lead magnet, quiz funnel, newsletter,
events, community, an offer, a service. Each one is a different door into the
same home.

**The three test questions** (ask them of any site; a "no" to any one is the
next collection point to build):
1. What can someone buy from you today, straight from your website?
2. What can someone who isn't ready to buy take for free, in exchange for
   staying in touch?
3. What would you sell next to someone who has already bought from you?

**Repurchase reality (as cited):** a 2024 study of 1,500+ Shopify stores found
only ~1 in 4 customers buys again. The packed cafe doesn't need every customer
to come back — it needs every customer to find *one more door*.

**One audience, many doors** — the nuance on Alex Hormozi's "one offer, one
avatar, one channel": the *focus* part stands (one audience, one problem —
coffee plus car insurance makes heads explode). But "one offer" was built for
the old world: AI can spin up new offers and landing pages fast (idea Monday →
live by Friday — the presenter's own team, mostly AI-built, shipped a new
collection point for their events business this way). Even Hormozi's newer
"100M Money Models" talks about a *sequence* of offers to the same customer.
Adding cross-sells doesn't need a new shop; every extra door is cheap to open
and is sold to people already in the room.

## 3. Why the old strategy broke (Extremistan)

**Mediocristan vs Extremistan** (Nassim Taleb): in Mediocristan no single person
moves the average (height); in Extremistan one entry swallows everything
(wealth). The internet *was* Mediocristan — a high street of little shops. Now
it's Extremistan: winner-takes-all, and AI picks the winners (one answer plus
citations instead of ten blue links). When power piles at the top, everyone
else becomes a tenant.

**The click collapse (as cited):** for every 1,000 Google searches in the US,
only ~360 clicks make it out to an external website — nearly 6 in 10 searches
already end without a click. AI is making this more extreme.

**Antifragility (Taleb's "Antifragile"):** individual restaurants are fragile;
the collective of restaurants is antifragile — each closure teaches the
survivors ("if no restaurant could ever fail, we'd all be eating cafeteria
food"). Goal: don't be the one restaurant (one menu, one bet — when it fails,
nothing is left); be the *food scene*. A failed smoothie launch at the packed
cafe doesn't close the cafe — they pull the smoothie, learn something about
the regulars, everything else carries on. Because data flows back to one owned
place, every failed experiment strengthens the other doors. This is also the
emotional cushion: with bread-and-butter offers bringing money in, a flopped
launch is a lesson, not a catastrophe.

## 4. The five layers

Every collection point — free guide or $10,000 program — sits on the same five
layers. Each layer only works if the one before it does.

| # | Layer | What it is | What good looks like (audit-facing) |
|---|-------|------------|------------------------------------|
| 1 | **Offer** | "The urgency gateway" — a promise to solve the most immediate problem. The aspirin to someone's headache. | One clear thing the audience wants, stated as an immediate-problem promise — not a vague menu of everything. |
| 2 | **Copy** | How the offer's value is communicated — copy that enters the conversation already happening in the visitor's head. | Page text speaks to the customer's actual headache and how the aspirin solves it — not self-description ("we are passionate about…"). |
| 3 | **Content** | The foot traffic — how people find the doors. Content is how people find you (as cited: 35% of 1,200 failed UK online startups cited lack of search visibility). | Regular public answers to the questions customers actually ask (sales calls, emails, DMs = content only the business can publish). |
| 4 | **Landing pages** | Every collection point gets a page with ONE job: get this specific buy. Homepage ≠ landing page: the homepage is the shopfront/hallway — it proves legit, points to the right counter (as cited: typical landing page converts ~6.6%; top quartile 11%+; the difference is one clear choice instead of five). | Single-focused pages per offer, each with one CTA — not multipurpose homepages doing a landing page's job. |
| 5 | **Funnel** | What happens to people not ready to buy (most of them): opt-in → value → offer, on autopilot. "The money is in the list": automated email (welcome series, abandoned cart) brings ~41% of email revenue from ~5% of sends (as cited: Klaviyo data). | A reason to come back: capture emails/attention in exchange for instant value; nurture cycles run without manual work; one clear ask at the end. |

**Conversion reference (as cited in the video):** organic social 2.2% vs paid
social 2.1%; organic search 4.9% vs paid search 5.4%; email (the owned list)
4.9%. Free traffic converts almost exactly like paid — only one of them sends
a bill. Traffic is rented; the list is owned.

## 5. AI-era discovery

**How AI recommends:** like a good friend asked "where should I get coffee?" —
one confident answer, not a list of ten. AI reaches for the names it keeps
seeing everywhere.

**Brand mentions beat backlinks (as cited):** Ahrefs studied 75,000 brands for
what predicts appearing in Google's AI answers — the strongest link was not
backlinks but *how the brand gets mentioned across the web*: shares, mentions
on other sites and platforms. YouTube is one of the biggest citation sources.
Every door opened is a signpost pointing back to the home; the more
collection points connect back to the one central place, the more the brand
gets mentioned, the more likely AI recommends it.

**Due diligence still happens:** even when AI (or a friend) introduces the
business, people check legitimacy before handing over money — is the site
updated, does the copy speak to them, are there resources? AI might introduce;
the digital home lets people verify in one place. FAQs on the site matter: they
answer exactly the questions AI looks for.

**AI traffic converts best (as cited):** AI-referred visitors convert at ~5.8%
(Ruler data) — the highest of any channel. AI might send fewer visitors, but
the ones it sends are the best-converting — and they need somewhere you own to
land.

**The presenter's proof story:** a client worth $35,000 USD found them via
ChatGPT ("find a boutique branding agency") — no prior references, purely
AI-referred, then closed through the site's contact flow.

**Case study of a home (the presenter's own site):** hallway = homepage (social
proof, case studies, FAQs); rooms = pages with one job each (an event page with
a single CTA sold out an entire event with no external marketing); roads in =
YouTube and Instagram channels pointing back; a school community page as an
extra collection point entering their value ladder; links back to the website
across every platform.

## 6. The value ladder

**Definition (Russell Brunson's map):** a series of offers at different price
points that all help the customer reach the same goal. Free taste at the
counter → coffee → lunch → run club → sauna → … the same customer, one step at
a time. Online: free content at the bottom → something small → course →
community → program → top-tier offer. The presenter's business runs from free
content and a $14 community up to a $100,000 offer.

**Rules:** most people stay at the bottom rung — that's fine; every rung is
another opportunity to say yes; you're not trying to make one thing work, you
are *raising the odds* that something works. Every rung increases lifetime
value and length of stay.

## 7. The person behind the system

The system amplifies whoever runs it. AI can hand you a Formula 1 car; you still
need to learn to drive. Knowing the audience, resilience through flops, and
emotional intelligence beat any website — a site can be cloned in 2 seconds;
the operator's skill can't. Building a business that turns a profit is a
developable skill: be in the arena, work through trial and error, build the
system *and* yourself alongside it.
